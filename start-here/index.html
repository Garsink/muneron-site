For companies using AI as a chat

From a chat window to an operating layer.

Most companies use AI the way they use a search box: one person, one question, one answer, nothing remembered. An operating layer is a different thing. It remembers the company, reads its systems, works inside written rules, and reports to the people who own each process.

This page is the map: what a layer is made of, what to have in place before day one, the order to build in, and what breaks first. All of it learned inside one real company, over four months, without a developer.

A chat is a brilliant intern with amnesia.

The model behind the chat has read most of the internet. It does one thing, predict the next word, and at that scale the trick becomes reading, writing and reasoning. That is the whole strength and the whole problem.

Brilliant at

Reading four hundred emails and saying which three matter. Drafting anything in seconds, in any language and tone. Spotting patterns people miss.

Unreliable at

Exact facts from memory. Your prices, your stock, your customers. Anything it cannot look up and verify. It will invent, confidently.

The fix

Plug it into your own systems, so it reads the truth instead of remembering it. Write the rules for anything that leaves the building. Give every output an owner. That is the layer.

The six parts.

Every operating layer, whatever the industry, is made of the same six parts. Missing any one of them is how a layer turns back into a chat.

It remembers the company

What happened across work mail, chats and meetings is logged and summarised by day and by week. Nobody re-explains the business every morning.

Rules it cannot break

Written rules for what leaves the building and who approves it. One stop switch that every job which sends or writes checks on every run. When in doubt, it stops.

It checks itself

The running jobs are recounted on a schedule. A silent job is reported, never assumed. Green on a status page is not proof that the work was done.

It learns from corrections

When a person corrects it, the lesson is written down where the next run will read it. Some lessons become hard blocks in code.

Staff get scoped helpers

Colleagues connect their own assistant with a personal key that reaches only the areas on their list. Health and personal data never route by job title.

The memory is yours

Company memory is plain files the company holds itself, readable by more than one AI tool. It is not locked to a vendor, and it does not vanish when a subscription ends.

Why a course does not get you there.

Most AI training covers two things: writing better prompts, and building a single assistant. Both are useful. Neither produces an operating layer, because a layer is not a prompt. It is memory, rules, connections and controls, built in an order, inside a real business, by someone who will live with the result. That part is rarely taught, because the people teaching it have usually not run it.

What one sentence produces.

An example from September 2026, with the company's name left out.

The owner of a food manufacturer, at the chat stage of AI, sent four files: two product catalogues and two slide decks. Asked for her biggest pains, she named three: daily KPIs, purchasing and cost, and information she could not find. That evening she had spent half an hour looking for her own company presentation and given up.

One sentence was given to the layer: review what she sent. It read the files, checked public sources, and by the end of the evening returned six documents: where to start on each of her topics, with an honesty label on every suggestion; the errors found in her own material, framed as checks before the next buyer pack; her company presentation redrafted from her own files, in her own style; the same presentation adapted for a retail chain, as an experiment; the architecture tour; and a three-minute film. The owner of the layer reviewed everything before it went out.

Her reply the next morning was one word long. What she asked for two days later was the list below.

Before day one: six things to have in place.

With these ready, the foundations of a layer take two to three days. Without them, weeks.

  1. 1
    One internal owner. A person inside the company who will work with the layer every day and say when it is wrong. Ideally the business owner.
  2. 2
    A one-page company map. Units, people and what each is responsible for, the main products, customer and supplier groups, the company's own terms. Hand-drawn is fine. The company memory is built from it.
  3. 3
    A list of every system where data lives. Mail, files, accounting, stock, production, tills, the web shop. Next to each: who administers it, and whether the data can be exported.
  4. 4
    An IT administrator, a computer that is always on, and an AI account on a work address. Without admin rights nothing connects. Training the administrator usually takes longest.
  5. 5
    Red lines. What the layer may never do: send outside the company, touch money, read anything personal. And who approves what.
  6. 6
    One weekly routine from each of three to five key people. What they do now, what they want, what "done" looks like, and the spreadsheet they keep by hand. The first automations come from these.

If the owner has access to the shared files, most of this list can be answered by running an audit with the AI itself. The owner drives; the layer reads. The first large piece of work in any company is that audit: the whole company's information, sorted onto shelves, with the relationships between them.

The order to build in.

The order followed inside the group, and the one change that would be made with hindsight.

  1. First
    Knowledge base. Company mail, chat and documents made searchable in one place. Context before anything else.
  2. Second
    The function that hurts most. For a trading business, incoming enquiries and quotes. For a manufacturer, likely purchasing and cost. One function, end to end, with a person approving the output.
  3. Same week
    Governance. A registry of every running job, a gate on outbound email, written rules. Inside the group this arrived three weeks after the first live sends. It belongs in the first week.
  4. Then
    Quality gates. A sanity check on every draft, an alert when a data feed stops, a supervisor over the jobs.
  5. Then
    Stop controls. One master stop over every job that can act, and a census of what is running, with the stale ones retired.
  6. Only then
    Automatic sending. Under explicit grants, with a floor, an opt-out per customer, and a log of every decision.
  7. Always
    Audit. A regular adversarial pass over every job, and a manual re-check of a sample of what the audit found. The group's own numbers.

What breaks first.

Not the model. The plumbing around it.

The report nobody reads

A job writes its output every day, on time, without error. Its only reader left months ago. The job is green. The work reaches nobody.

The feed that stopped

A data feed dies quietly. The processing chain keeps running on schedule, stamping today's date on months-old input. Every status stays ok.

The control on the wrong machine

A guard is tested and verified, on a machine that is not the one talking to customers. The one that is runs without it.

The rules that fell behind

The business approves new things faster than the written rules are updated. Within weeks the rulebook describes a system that no longer exists.

This is why "it checks itself" is one of the six parts, and why the first thing built after the knowledge base should be a way to see whether a job did its work, not whether it finished.

Four things owners assume.

"It needs programmers and a big IT project."
Every function in the group started as a routine task described in plain words. What it needs is one owner who checks the output and says when it is wrong.
"It is there to replace people."
Same team, more handled. The layer takes the copy-paste half. People keep the judgment and the relationships.
"It reads everyone's private messages."
Company mailboxes, company systems and the work chats it is a member of, all logged. No personal accounts, phones or private chats.
"AI is always right."
It makes mistakes, including confident ones. That is why people check its work, set its limits and hold the stop switch.
