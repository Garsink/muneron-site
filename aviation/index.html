What I built, in the order I built it, and where it failed.

I run an aviation parts, MRO and landing gear leasing group. I am not a developer. Everything below was built by describing what I wanted, checking what came back, and describing again. Four months of building, 1,650 commits across 15 repositories, and not one line typed by a developer.

It started as one question typed into a tool late one night about my own company. I never closed the window. Nothing was designed up front. It was added to, one sentence at a time, which is the only way someone who cannot code could have built it.

The order tells the story.

Sales throughput first. Governance second, retrofitted. Verification third, after silent failures piled up. The audit last.

  1. April
    The knowledge vault first. Every email, chat and document the company produces, made readable in one place. Context before anything else.
  2. May
    The revenue engines. Reading incoming enquiries, drafting priced quotes, onboarding paperwork, live reads of the ERP and the books, and a decision queue for me.
  3. 31 May
    The entire governance layer, in one day. A registry of everything that runs, a gate on every outbound email, a written charter. Retrofitted three weeks after the first live sends, because uncontrolled outbound was the first real fear.
  4. June
    Quality gates and self-healing. A sanity gate on every draft, alerts when a feed dies quietly, a supervisor over the engines. Silent failure had become the enemy.
  5. July
    Brakes and audits. One master brake over everything that can act. A census of every agent, with the stale ones retired the same day. An auto-send pilot under explicit grants only. Regression evals.
  6. August
    Hardening. A fail-closed brake rule. A dedicated customer-facing machine. The records engine frozen and rebuilt. Starter packages so colleagues could install their own desk. The rulebook rewritten to match what was actually running.
  7. September
    The audit of the whole fleet. Then the audit of the audit. Both are on the front page.

One layer, four laws.

It is one layer that reads across everything the company does and routes the few things that matter to whoever owns them. It is not a dashboard. Every part of it obeys four laws I wrote down before it obeyed them.

  1. Understand and connect. Not just detect. The output is meaning, signals tied into a picture, never a loose line on a pile.
  2. Choose and route. Decide the few things that matter and send each to the person who owns that process. Volume is the failure, not the goal.
  3. Verify against the live source. Nothing surfaces unless it was checked against the system of record that minute and shows where it came from. When the data is wrong, it says so and routes the fix. It does not act on it.
  4. Close the loop to a real person. Every recommendation reaches its owner. For actions: propose, approve, execute, confirm. Nothing dies silently.

What it does, by function.

The human decides. The layer reads, drafts, checks and chases. Where a line crosses out of the building, it does so through a named lane, below.

Customer enquiries and quotes

Reads every incoming request, prices it against stock and history, drafts the quote. Some offers leave automatically under rules we set, and any customer can be flagged manual-only.

Purchasing and suppliers

Watches what needs buying, chases suppliers for offers, and writes the offers back into the system so nothing is asked twice.

Finance

Keeps a live picture of cash, receivables and payables, and refuses to put a money figure in front of anyone unless it was pulled fresh from the source that minute. Bank before books, always.

People

Handles staff questions and paperwork, with one hard stop: health and personal data never route by job title, to anyone.

Market and early warning

Reads the market and the region every morning and reports the few things that actually changed, with the source next to each.

Meetings

Turns recordings into minutes and chases the follow-ups afterwards. Names who attended from the evidence, not from the invitation.

Marketing

Feeds marketing the real signals from inside the company, stripped of numbers and names, each one marked with what a person must verify before anything is published.

Knowledge

An append-only ledger of what happened across mail, chat, meetings and deals, kept in lanes, so that private things can never reach a shared briefing.

The immune system

Watchdogs over the watchers. A recipient guard. A scope gate that applies the lowest permission in any group. A guard on payment language. The ERP read-only by default. And a graded audit of itself.

What leaves the building.

It reads almost everything. It acts outward through exactly four lanes. Each exists because I authorised it, each has a gate, each has its own off switch, and every send is logged.

  1. Email to colleagues. Capped per person per day and in total, scrubbed for confidentiality, every send in the daily digest.
  2. Priced quotes to customers. Armed only while a specific file is present, disarmed the moment it is not. A floor under every quote. Any account can opt out to manual.
  3. Quote submissions to the parts marketplace. Its own off switch.
  4. Public stock listings. Must be explicitly armed, daily cap, its own pause.
One master brake stops all four at once.

Two rules I typed that have no exceptions: it does not sign, and it does not move money. And reports to me alone are deliberately not gated, because I must keep receiving the alert that everything else has stopped.

Where it failed.

The costliest mistakes were not the AI making things up. They were plain code, silent defaults and drift between machines.

A guard was verified live on the wrong machine, so the machine that actually talked to customers ran without it. A processing chain kept running on schedule after its raw feed died, stamping today's date on months-old input, and nothing errored. The rulebook fell behind the system it was meant to govern: several things the company had approved were not yet written in it.

So in August I rewrote the rules to match what was running, and made every engine that can act announce itself to the brake on every run, with a separate check that reports any that do not.

On 2 September I put the whole fleet through an adversarial audit: 311 evaluations, 102 places where the job finished clean, showed green, and was wrong or reached nobody. The first repair wave went in the same week. On 20 September I took twenty of those 102 at random and re-verified them by hand against the original logs. Nineteen held. One did not, and it is on the front page.

Since this week, every prediction the layer or I make gets written down with a date and the artefact that will settle it. Nothing is graded yet. A ledger is worth nothing until it is.

What it is not.

  • Not a product you can download. It was built for one company, by its owner, against that company's systems.
  • Not a platform, and not one ecosystem. Four machines, several code states, and an honest gap between them that the audit named.
  • Not self-learning. It remembers and it obeys. It does not yet learn from its own outcomes, and I will not say it does until the ledger shows it.

If you are running this too, I would like to hear what broke.

No deck, no proposal. Tell me what you run and what you cannot account for.
